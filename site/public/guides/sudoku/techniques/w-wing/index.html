W-Wing · Step-by-step example

Two nonseeing cells hold the same pair {X,Y}; a strong link on X has one end seen by each wing. Remove the other pair digit from a candidate seeing both wings.

Recognize the pattern

Two nonseeing cells hold the same pair {X,Y}; a strong link on X has one end seen by each wing.

How to find it

Find matching pairs in cells that do not see each other, then trace an exact two-place link for one pair digit through a row, column, or box.

What it allows

Remove the other pair digit from a candidate seeing both wings.

Avoid the mix-up

The matching pair alone is not enough. Check both link ends, its exact two-place condition, and the target’s sight of both wings.

Step-by-step example · Strong row

This example starts from a saved intermediate position. Its candidates include earlier logical eliminations; they are not fresh automatic notes.

Blue: evidence. Green: conclusion. Crossed-out digits: eliminated candidates. R1C1 means row 1, column 1.

Step 1 · This is a W-Wing

Start with R4C8 and R6C3: both contain only 9 and 6, so they are the two wings.

Step 2 · See the complete W-Wing

Each wing sees one end of the 6 strong link. In row 5, 6 can go only in R5C8 and R5C3, so one must be 6. Every target (R6C8=9) sees both wings.

Step 3 · Case 1: R5C8 is 6

If R5C8=6, it rules out 6 from R4C8. That wing must be 9. Since R6C8=9 sees R4C8, it cannot be 9.

Step 4 · Case 2: R5C3 is 6

If R5C3=6, it rules out 6 from R6C3. That wing must be 9. Since R6C8=9 sees R6C3, it cannot be 9.

Step 5 · Combine the two cases

The two cases cover the strong link, so at least one wing is 9. Every target sees both wings; remove R6C8=9.

What it allows
